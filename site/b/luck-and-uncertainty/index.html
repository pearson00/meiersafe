Luck and uncertainty: how luck smears the outcome

Key idea

Our safety level depends on technical skill and on judgment, and the more unknowns a task has, the more judgment matters. Luck "smears" where we end up, and smears it most when unknowns are many. Reducing unknowns improves safety and also makes our analysis of what happened more accurate.

By Mike Meier · First published November 12, 2026 (planned) · Adapted from "Lucky or Good – Further Thoughts," Flight Test Safety Fact, Issue 21-09, September 2021

Skill and luck #

In the May 2021 issue of Flight Test Safety Fact, Art "Turbo" Tomassetti illustrated a favorite theme of his with a colored chart showing how skill and luck interact to determine performance. His point: I want both, … but I can only make one. So the best thing any of us can do … is to try to increase our skill.

An obvious thought, one might say, and yet the way Turbo says it, it really hits home. It also made Mike think a lot about luck, and about the role luck plays in our performance, our safety, and our evaluation of both.

Skill, judgment and unknowns #

Mike's variation on Turbo's chart makes three changes:

  • Skill is split in two: technical skill on the left axis, and judgment, the quality of evaluation and decision making, on the right.
  • Performance becomes safety level, the aspect of performance he is drawn to.
  • A third variable runs left to right: the degree of uncertainty, or the number and significance of unknowns.

Place a point for technical skill on the left side and one for judgment on the right, and draw a vertical line whose position shows how much is unknown. The expected safety level is where the two lines cross.

With few unknowns, the vertical line sits to the left and technical skill is the overriding factor. As unknowns increase and the line moves right, the quality of judgment becomes more and more the determining factor.

Luck as smearing #

Where does luck fit? Mike ended up with the thought that luck is a kind of analogue to "quantum smearing," the effect by which tiny particles like electrons have no precise location, only a smeared probability of where they might be.

Luck means we can no longer count on the intersection of the two lines to locate our safety level precisely. It smears the intersection, and differently depending on the unknowns:

Few unknowns
Technical skill predominates, the opportunity for luck is reduced, and the smearing is minimal.
Many unknowns
Judgment predominates, and the opportunity for luck to change the outcome is greatly increased. The smearing can drag the outcome down into the caution region, or lift it above what our judgment deserved.

Luck in the analysis afterwards #

These effects feed back into our post-event analysis too. Where luck has more chance to affect the result, we are more likely to misjudge the quality of the skill or judgment we brought to the task. That is Murphy's Corollary again, from a different direction.

Three things we can do up front #

  1. Increase our skill.
  2. Improve the quality of our decision making.
  3. Reduce the number and significance of unknowns. This one has an extra benefit: by reducing the room for luck, it also makes our analysis afterwards of the quality of our decisions more accurate.
