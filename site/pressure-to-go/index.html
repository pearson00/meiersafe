The pressure to go: the hardest factor to overcome

Key idea

Deadlines, budgets, expectations and our own ego give us a strong incentive to go even when we have doubts. A successful go relieves the pressure, so we are quick to call it a good decision. A no-go only increases the pressure next time. Either way, the reinforcement teaches the wrong lesson.

Where the pressure comes from #

If all of the foregoing were not enough, there is one very significant additional factor: the inevitable pressure on us to make the go decision.

This pressure can take many forms and have many sources: an impending deadline, a project that is behind schedule or over budget or below expectations, or simply our own personal ego. But it is almost always present in some form. The result is that we have a strong incentive to make the go decision even when we have some level of doubt about it.

Reinforced the wrong way, both ways #

Beyond that, we have a strengthened tendency to validate the go decision as a good one when the outcome is successful, because now the pressure has been relieved.

Conversely, if we make the no-go decision, the pressure to go next time typically only increases, as we are now farther behind on whatever schedule, budget or other factor created the pressure in the first place.

Below: from the 2018 Gulfstream talk, not in the paper.

After a go that succeeds by good fortune
We may be positively reinforced for what was, in the end, a bad decision.
After a good no-go
We are likely to be negatively reinforced, by the increased pressure, for having made the right call.

Either kind of inappropriate reinforcement will harm the quality of our future decisions.

Not immune: February 3, 2015 #

In the paper, an indented aside: material beyond the talk

I have, over time, come to the conclusion that the pressure to go may be the single most difficult factor to overcome in the pursuit of safety. I have observed in my own decision making that I am far from immune to it, despite the extent of the analysis I have done into its potential for negatively impacting the decision making process in relation to safety.

Below: from the 2018 Gulfstream talk, not in the paper.

Seven weeks before he was due to present the paper for the first time, at the SETP West Coast Section Symposium in March 2015, Mike launched at sunset in a light wind crossing down the slope, to get the footage that would certify a competition glider in time for the World Championships. The launch failed. He was unhurt; the glider came away with a broken downtube.

I was a lot luckier than I deserved to be.

Pressure in the Challenger decision #

In the paper, an indented aside: material beyond the talk

The existence or perception of pressure is referenced a number of times in the Rogers Commission's report on Challenger. The commission noted, for example:

14 William P. Rogers et al., Report of the Presidential Commission on the Space Shuttle Challenger Accident (Washington, DC, June 6, 1986), ch. IX, sec. VII, "Flight Rate."
The nation's reliance on the Shuttle as its principal space launch capability created a relentless pressure on NASA to increase the flight rate.
15 Ibid., ch. V.

Roger Boisjoly, a structures engineer at Thiokol who strongly opposed the launch of Challenger under the temperature conditions at the time, testified: I felt personally that management was under a lot of pressure to launch and that they made a very tough decision, but I didn't agree with it.

Real or perceived, the effect is the same #

In the paper, an indented aside: material beyond the talk

Interestingly, it does not really matter whether pressure is real or merely perceived; it has the same effect. In Moonshot, Alan Shepard and Deke Slayton wrote of Skip Chauvin's decision to decline an engineer's suggestion to cancel the plugs-out test because of multiple problems:

16 Alan Shepard and Deke Slayton, Moonshot (Atlanta: Turner Publishing, 1994), 198.
Time was more important. It was becoming damned critical with all the pressures mounting to get this ship on its way into orbit.
