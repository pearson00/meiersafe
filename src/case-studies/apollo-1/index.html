Apollo 1: a history of success with pure oxygen

Key idea

A long run of successes with pure oxygen in the capsule made a known fire hazard look safe. There had been previous fires in such environments and documentation of the hazard. The Senate committee concluded that the history of success had led to overconfidence and complacency.

At a glance

Date
January 27, 1967
Outcome
Fire in the spacecraft during the plugs-out test at Launch Complex 34; the three crew, Virgil Grissom, Edward White and Roger Chaffee, were killed.
History of success
A pressurized pure oxygen environment in manned space capsules.
Warning signs
Previous fires in such environments, and documentation of fire hazards in pure oxygen.
What was not recognized
A complex electrical system in a significantly pressurized pure oxygen environment was a dangerous condition.
Sources quoted
Frank Borman's testimony before the House Subcommittee on NASA Oversight; the report of the Senate Committee on Aeronautical and Space Sciences; Shepard and Slayton's Moonshot.

Below, through "Not recognized as hazardous": Mike's text from the paper, pp. 16–17, with the testimony and report it quotes.

A history of success #

We’d had a history of success with a pressurized pure oxygen environment in manned space capsules, until Apollo One. Had we thought sufficiently critically about it, we might have concluded that a complex electrical system in a significantly pressurized pure oxygen environment was a dangerous condition. We had even had previous fires in such environments, and we might have drawn on those as evidence of such danger.

Not recognized as hazardous #

But, in the words of Frank Borman, the astronauts’ representative on the Apollo 204 Review Board, in testimony before the House Subcommittee on NASA Oversight,

Paper, note 5 Investigation Into Apollo 204 Accident: Hearings Before the Subcommittee on NASA Oversight of the Committee on Science and Astronautics, U.S. House of Representatives, Ninetieth Congress, April 10, 1967, Evening Session, history.nasa.gov, accessed September 5, 2015, http://history.nasa.gov/Apollo204/bormanhouse1.pdf, 81.
I don’t believe that any of us recognized that the test conditions for this test were hazardous.
Paper, note 6 Ibid., 87.
We did not think, and this is a failing on my part and on everyone associated with us; we did not recognize the fact that we had the three essentials, an ignition source, extensive fuel and, of course, we knew we had the oxygen.

In the Summary of its report, the Committee on Aeronautical and Space Sciences of the United States Senate had this to say:

Paper, note 7 Clinton P. Anderson et al., "Apollo 204 Accident: Report of the Committee on Aeronautical and Space Sciences, United States Senate, Report N. 956," NASA Historical Reference Collection, NASA History Office, NASA Headquarters, Washington, DC, January 30, 1968, http://history.nasa.gov/as204_senate_956.pdf, 9, 10.
It is clear from the Board’s report and the testimony before the committee that this kind of accident was completely unexpected despite the amount of documentation of fire hazards in pure oxygen environments. The committee can only conclude that NASA's long history of successes in testing and launching space vehicles with pure oxygen environments at 16.7 p.s.i. and lower pressures led to overconfidence and complacency.

The pressure to go #

Below: from the paper's section on the pressure to go, not its Apollo One text.

In the paper, an indented aside: material beyond the talk

Interestingly, it does not really matter whether pressure is real or merely perceived; it has the same effect. In Moonshot, Alan Shepard and Deke Slayton wrote of Skip Chauvin's decision to decline an engineer's suggestion to cancel the plugs-out test because of multiple problems:

Paper, note 16 Alan Shepard and Deke Slayton, Moonshot (Atlanta: Turner Publishing, 1994), 198.
Time was more important. It was becoming damned critical with all the pressures mounting to get this ship on its way into orbit.

More on this factor: Factor III · The pressure to go.

Which factors it shows #

I · Deceptive mathematics
A long history of successes felt like evidence that pure oxygen at pressure was safe. A string of successes is exactly what a small risk per test produces, so it says little about the risk itself.
II · Murphy's Corollary
Nothing had gone wrong, so nothing could go wrong. Previous fires and the documented hazard were there to read, yet in Borman's words no one recognized that the test conditions were hazardous.
III · The pressure to go
In Mike's words, the pressure to go "was absolutely a factor in the Apollo 1 accident": the program was competing feverishly with the Soviet Union, under President Kennedy's goal of landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to Earth before the end of the decade. In Moonshot's account, the plugs-out test went ahead despite multiple problems because "time was more important."

Questions for discussion #

  1. Which condition in your test program has been present on every success so far, and has never been treated as a hazard?
  2. What earlier incidents, in your program or in others, point at a danger you have not yet experienced yourself?
  3. Who on your team would say "these test conditions are hazardous," and when did anyone last say it?
  4. Where has a long run of success in your program turned into overconfidence or complacency?
  5. Estimate the chance that one of your tests ends in an accident, then use the calculator to see what that chance becomes over all the tests your program has run so far. Does your run of successes still read as evidence of safety?
